How to Lose $50 Million with Iron Condors

A look at how a defined-risk options strategy became a reported $50 million loss.

In December 2025, a group of traders following David Chau, known online as “Captain Condor,” suffered a reported loss of roughly $50 million. They traded iron condors on the S&P 500 index, collecting premiums in exchange for betting that the index would stay within a chosen range.

Each position had a maximum loss per spread. But after losses, the group increased its size. By Christmas Eve, the position had grown so large that a modest move in the index pushed the final trade into its maximum-loss zone.

This article looks at how the trade worked, how the position grew, and why a defined-risk spread can still put an account at risk.

A note on the numbers

The trade sizes and credits below come from a public reconstruction by VolSignals. They describe estimated group activity, not verified fills for every participant. Individual results depended on entry prices, size, fees, and whether traders closed their positions early.

The estimated position grew 25 times from the first trade to the last. At the same time, the range in which the group could keep the full credit narrowed from 95 index points to 30.

How an iron condor works

An iron condor combines a put spread below the market and a call spread above it.

The seller collects a credit when opening the position. If SPX finishes between the two short strikes, the options expire without intrinsic value and the seller keeps the credit, before costs.

The long options cap the loss on either side. For a five-point-wide SPX condor, the maximum loss is $500 per spread minus the credit received, before fees. SPX options use a $100 multiplier. Cboe explains the payoff and contract terms.

The martingale strategy

The reported strategy used a martingale-style progression: increase the next position after a losing trade in an attempt to recover prior losses.

A traditional martingale assumes an even-money bet. These condors did not offer even-money payouts. On the final trade, a seller risked about $355 to make $145 per spread, before costs. That meant a winning trade needed enough size to cover more than one dollar of prior losses for every dollar of potential profit.

The group increased its exposure as the losses accumulated. The approach also depended on the next winning trade arriving before traders ran out of capital or the credit received fell too far.

The final trade

For December 24, the reported condor had short strikes at 6,890 and 6,920, with protective options five points farther out.

Using the approximate $1.45 credit and 110,000 spreads, the maximum profit was $15.95 million and the maximum loss was $39.05 million, before costs. These are calculations for the estimated aggregate position, not the result of any one trader.

VolSignals estimated that after costs, the group risked about $40.15 million to make $14.85 million. Its reconstruction put cumulative effective losses before the final trade at roughly $22.7 million. On those assumptions, even a full win on the final position would not have recovered all prior losses.

SPX closed around 6,932 on December 24, above the 6,925 long call strike. A trader holding this condor to expiration would therefore have reached the maximum-loss zone on the call side. Options strategist Lawrence McMillan’s account also describes the strikes and closing outcome.

A high win rate does not tell the whole story

A trader can win on most days and still lose money overall. The size of the wins, the size of the losses, and the number of contracts all matter.

For the final trade’s simplified payoff, the maximum win was $145 per spread and the maximum loss was $355. Ignoring costs and partial outcomes, the trader would need to win 71% of the time to break even in a model with only maximum wins and maximum losses.

That 71% is a teaching example, not the trade’s actual probability of profit. Real outcomes can fall between the maximum win and maximum loss, and the probability changes with market conditions and entry prices.

What this trade can and cannot tell us

The positions were large enough to attract attention from market participants. Dealer hedging and market liquidity may influence price action, but the public reconstruction does not establish that a particular counterparty deliberately moved SPX to trigger the condor.

Nor does this case prove that every iron condor or short-dated options strategy will fail. It shows a specific vulnerability: increasing exposure after losses can make a capped loss per contract overwhelming at the portfolio level.

The questions I would ask before entering a similar trade are:

  1. How much can I lose across the entire position, including costs?
  2. How does the next position change if this trade loses?
  3. Could I withstand several losses in a row without increasing risk?
  4. Does the potential profit on the next trade cover the losses I’m trying to recover?
  5. What will I do if the index reaches a short strike before expiration?

An iron condor defines the payoff of the spread. The trader still chooses how many spreads to sell.
